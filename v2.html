The Same Countries, Different Rules

Every African country's first head of state after independence and the one in office today, on one map. I compiled the dataset myself. Each ring is five years in power, and the stem shows whether power changed hands by election or by force.

Tableau · custom cartography · 2026Open on Tableau Public ↗

The coup belt

Mali, Burkina Faso, Chad, Sudan and Niger were all republics at independence. All five are under military rule today.

Same man, new rules

Uganda was a republic in 1962 and has been authoritarian since 1986, under the same person for forty years.

The longest reign

Teodoro Obiang has governed Equatorial Guinea for 555 months. That is ten full rings, more than anyone else on the continent.

  1. The question

    Did the systems of government African countries carried out of independence survive, and who is still standing in them?

  2. The data

    A dataset I built by hand: administration type, mode of entry, exit type and the dates each leader was sworn in and out.

  3. The build

    It isn't a Tableau map. It's a fitted outline of Africa with one hand-placed coordinate per country, and every mark is drawn with MAKEPOINT and MAKELINE from calculated coordinates.

  4. The result

    The coup belt reads as one block, and one ring count runs longer than every other on the continent.

TrackPerform

Coaches upload a GPS export from any vendor, in almost any shape, and get a per-athlete readiness read in seconds.

Live
trackperform.com ↗
When

Role
Data model, product design, full-stack build

It started as a competition entry

Chelsea FC released a set of GPS tracking data and asked people to analyse it. I built the dashboard in Tableau: player load, acute versus chronic training demand, a match-day calendar and a full player profile. It was shortlisted. It didn't win, but it taught me exactly what a load dashboard has to say, and to whom.

The message that made it a product

A physical trainer at a top-flight European club had seen it and asked for the same thing built on his squad's data. When it was done he asked whether it could build itself: a coach uploads their export, whatever its shape, and the analysis comes back on its own.

I'd built it twice by hand, so I knew every step between a raw export and a finished read. The open question was whether AI could handle the messy middle, reading a file it had never seen and working out what each column meant. It could.

Then the real files arrived

Around forty coaches and sport scientists signed up. Everyone's spreadsheet was different. Some packed a week into one sheet as stacked tables. Others uploaded a session with no dates, because to them the date was obvious. Uploads bounced, and the ones that worked took two to three minutes to show anything. People uploaded once, watched a spinner and left.

The fix

I rebuilt the intake on one assumption: no two files agree. AI now reads the raw export and works out its structure: where the data starts, which column is the date, and whether the sheet is one day or a season in blocks. If a day has no date, it asks. If a date is ambiguous, it settles US versus European format instead of guessing. Then I removed the wait. The dashboard returns your first seven days in a couple of seconds and streams the rest in behind you.

DrillCal

A coaching journal for planning the week: drill library, calendar, tactical board and attendance. The playbook belongs to the coach, not the club.

Live
drillcal.com ↗
Launched
1 Aug 2026 · 30+ signups
Role
Data model, calendar UX, build
Stack
React · Supabase · Python · Claude

What coaches kept saying

Their work was scattered across hard drives, Google Drive, WhatsApp threads and laptops with no backup. And session videos often belong to the club, so they're gone the day a coach leaves. One rule was settled before anything was designed: the library belongs to the coach's account. Change clubs, import your drills, carry on. Ten years and five teams later it's all still there.

The calendar broke where it mattered

Drag a session to another part of the day and its duration vanished. A four-hour block dropped into the morning came back with no start or end and stretched across all six hours. An all-day item couldn't be dragged into a time, and a period could hold only one session, so a day with two sessions couldn't be planned at all.

One fix for all three

The calendar treated the slot as the truth and the times as decoration. I inverted that. Duration now travels with the session, a period holds as many sessions as the day has, and an all-day item drops into a time like anything else. Underneath, Postgres row-level security means a shared team never means a shared library.
